# Abstract
The development of next-generation supersonic transport (SST) represents a critical direction for future civil aviation. However, most existing SST configurations feature small passenger capacity, which greatly limits their commercial application potential. The fuselage volume of the conventional tube-and-wing (TAW) configuration increases quadratically with seats. To address this issue, a 160-seat large-sized supersonic blended-wing-body (SBWB) configuration is proposed in this paper, and a dedicated parametric method is established to generate the baseline configuration under cabin volume constraints. To characterize the sonic boom performance of the SBWB, systematic analyses are conducted on both fore-body and rear-body waveform. The Jones–Seebass–George–Darden (JSGD) inverse design method is adopted for fore-body waveform optimization, reducing the ground perceived loudness level (PLdB) of the SBWB by 6.46. Rear-body waveform analysis reveals prominent over-expansion phenomena for large-sized SBWB, which restricts further sonic boom reduction. Therefore, the regulation effect of aerodynamic auxiliary components on the rear-body waveform is investigated. The results indicate that winglets can weaken the intensity of rear-body over-expansion and deliver waveform regulation benefits to a certain degree, while the control effect of V-tails is rather limited, the sonic boom intensity is further reduced to 98.7PLdB. Overall, the 160-seat SBWB exhibits aerodynamic advantages including a low cruise angle of attack and a broad lift distribution and demonstrates promising low-boom potential at large passenger capacity, providing a reference for the low-boom design of future large-sized SST configurations.

# 1.    Introduction
Faster travel speed is an eternal pursuit of humanity. The flight speed of supersonic transport is twice that of traditional subsonic airliners or even higher, which can greatly improve travel efficiency and facilitate global economic and cultural exchanges. Hence, the new-generation SST represents a core technological frontier in the aviation industry of the 21st century[1-2]. In its aviation strategic plan released in 2023, NASA stated that "the development of efficient, economical and eco-friendly supersonic commercial transportation will become a gamechanger for intercontinental travel in the future"[3]. In 2025, the White House signed a presidential executive order titled "Leading the World in Supersonic Flight" to boost the development of domestic supersonic civil air transportation.

After decades of research, various countries have developed some advanced low-boom SST configurations, such as NASA X-59[4], Lockheed Martin QSTA[5], TsAGI Strizh of Russia[6] and JAXA S4 of Japan[7]. Nevertheless, most of these designs target small-capacity supersonic business jets. The X-59, which delivers the optimal sonic boom suppression, carries just a single passenger and serves merely as a demonstrator for sonic boom research. While small supersonic business jet concepts enjoy certain market demand and can more readily satisfy low sonic boom limits, supersonic transport imposes stringent airport requirements and consume far more fuel than subsonic airliners, leading to exorbitant operating costs. To offset such heavy costs, airfares would inevitably surge, making these aircraft inaccessible to most travelers. Expanding seating capacity stands out as one of the most straightforward approaches to boosting economic efficiency. Distributing fixed operational expenditures including airport fees and fuel costs across a larger passenger pool can cut ticket prices while lifting overall economic returns. Over the past few decades, research into low-boom design has matured steadily within academic circles. Geometry shaping stands as the most widely adopted and effective design technique[2]. Driven by the demands of aerodynamic configuration design, a set of powerful inverse design algorithms including JSGD[8-11], LSG[12-14] and PNFO[15] have been developed. These advances make the low-boom design of large-sized SST a viable prospect.

Fuselage of TAW configurations suffers from low volumetric efficiency. When adopted for large-sized supersonic airliners, stringent volumetric constraints drastically raise the complexity of low-boom design. Innovative configurations for supersonic civil aircraft have also been investigated in recent years, among which the bidirectional flying wing configuration[16-17] is a representative example. However, its rotating mechanism creates unprecedented structural hurdles for the passenger cabin and fails to deliver tangible improvements in commercial profitability in practice. By contrast, the blended-wing-body (BWB) configuration largely leverages the proven component architecture of conventional aircraft[18-19]. Beyond its superior aerodynamic performance, the non-circular central fuselage of the BWB conforms closely to cabin contours, which partially relaxes the volumetric penalties incurred by increased seating capacity and facilitate low-boom optimization.

Accordingly, a 160-seat large SBWB configuration is proposed in this study. Its aerodynamic and sonic boom characteristics are analyzed, and JSGD inverse design method is applied to fore-body waveform design. After subsequent rear-body waveform assessment, the effects of winglets and V-tails on wave profiles are investigated.

# 2.    Methodology

## 2.1    Aerodynamic analysis

Aerodynamic and high-fidelity sonic boom analyses in this study are performed based on high-precision CFD numerical approaches. The Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations combined with the  turbulence model serve as the governing equations. Zonal meshes composed of cylindrical near-field and conical far-field domains are adopted for high-fidelity sonic boom calculation. Grid lines of the conical mesh are arranged along the Mach angle to suppress numerical dissipation in sonic boom propagation.

The JAXA-Wing-Body (JWB) standard model from the 2nd AIAA Sonic Boom Prediction Workshop (SBPW) is employed for numerical method validation. Figure 1 presents the geometric configuration and sonic boom mesh topology of the JWB model, with a total mesh cell count of 17 million.

Calculation outcomes are compared with data contributed by workshop participants (Figure 2). The near-field pressure waveform achieves favorable consistency with reference results. Discrepancies observed in rear-body waveform primarily originate from intricate local flow features, which induce noticeable deviations among different research outputs. Even so, the overall variation tendency of obtained results aligns well with published datasets. Aerodynamic force predictions also show satisfactory agreement with official benchmark values. The validation confirms the reliability of the adopted CFD method, which can be applied to further detailed flow and wave characteristic analysis.

## 2.2    Sonic Boom Prediction

The sonic boom calculation methods applied in this study are divided into two types. The low-fidelity modified Whitham linear theory is utilized for rapid low-boom design. The far-field propagation method based on the Augmented Burgers equation is integrated with the near-field overpressure waveform calculated by CFD, allowing high-fidelity sonic boom analysis covering both near and far fields.

_2.2.1_    _Rapid Sonic Boom Prediction_

The JWB model is adopted to validate the rapid sonic boom prediction method derived from modified Whitham linear theory. The corresponding F-function is calculated from equivalent-area distribution to acquire near-field sonic boom characteristics, while lift distribution is solved using the high-order panel method. Comparisons between predicted signatures and CFD outcomes are presented in Figure 3. Results from the rapid method follow basically identical variation trends with high-fidelity calculations, and the attained accuracy satisfies design demands focused on forebody low-boom optimization.

# 3.    Baseline SBWB Configuration

## 3.1    Parametric Method and Baseline Configuration Generation

The TAW fuselage diameter is constrained by cabin size, and its sectional area grows quadratically with the number of seats per row. For large SSTs, this leads to a substantial increase in fuselage volume and raises the difficulty of sonic boom design. In comparison, the BWB adopts a non-circular fuselage that better fits the cabin, providing a more compact volume distribution and lower volume penalty at the same seating capacity (Figure 5). However, the high integration of the cabin and wing makes the traditional split design framework inapplicable, thereby requiring a full-aircraft parametric method dedicated to the SBWB configuration.

In view of this issue, a full-aircraft CST parametric method (Figure 6) is adopted in this study to guarantee the global geometric continuity and smoothness of the configuration during optimization. In this approach, the SBWB geometry is defined by a set of closed sectional curves, where all sections are parameterized using CST functions of identical order. Third-order NURBS interpolation is employed between adjacent sections to construct aerodynamic surfaces and maintain second-order geometric continuity. Additional design parameters cover the streamwise coordinates of leading/trailing edges as well as the vertical offset of each section. Low-boom design implemented within this unified parametric framework avoids repeated FFD box reconstruction and re-parameterization induced by geometric deformation.

During surface generation, CST parameters are constructed based on Bernstein polynomials with infinite-order differentiability everywhere, which preserves the G2 continuity of streamwise grids for all sections. Similarly, cubic NURBS surfaces can theoretically achieve inherent G2 continuity without extra modification. Accordingly, the present parametric method enables full-aircraft surfaces to maintain continuous G2 characteristics throughout the configuration. On this basis, additional constraints are imposed on the spanwise surface geometry to avoid structurally infeasible shapes in engineering practice. Total curvature variation and second-derivative inflection criteria are adopted in this work as practical engineering constraints.

The cabin envelope is established as the primary geometric constraint for SBWB parametric modeling. A 160-seat layout with 40 rows and four seats per row is configured, and major cabin dimensions are specified to meet passenger comfort demands. Due to the limited internal space of supersonic transport, overhead luggage racks are eliminated to prevent spatial crowding. Baggage storage is arranged in the tapered section of the rear fuselage instead.

The longitudinal symmetry-plane contour is generated according to the cabin boundary dimensions. The overall aircraft length is reasonably configured with a reserved height margin to compensate for geometric shrinkage in the spanwise direction. Key control points at the front, rear, upper and lower boundaries of the cabin symmetry plane are selected, and a CST curve is fitted to construct the baseline profile. During the baseline configuration design, offsets are applied to the nose and tail to increase the effective aircraft length, providing a good geometric foundation for subsequent low-boom optimization.

A 3%-thickness thin airfoil is applied uniformly to the outboard wing sections away from the cabin. The sectional profiles of the middle fuselage are acquired by interpolating the airfoil parameters between the outboard wing and the symmetry plane, and the initial aerodynamic configuration is thus constructed.

Considering flight efficiency and sonic boom characteristics, the cruise condition adopted in this study is set as a cruise altitude of 18 km and a cruise Mach number of 1.8, and the total weight of SBWB is 150t. To match the lift of the shape with the design weight, the leading-edge curve is further parameterized by a cubic polynomial, and the rapid lift-increasing optimization is carried out on the initial shape, thereby obtaining the baseline configuration of this paper (Figure 8).

## 3.2    Aerodynamic Analysis of the Baseline Configuration

Aerodynamic and sonic boom characteristics of the baseline configuration are analyzed in this paper. As the panel method serves as the lift assessment tool in primary rapid optimization, the cruise angle of attack is firstly evaluated via high-fidelity calculation with relatively coarse aerodynamic mesh. The sonic boom mesh is then generated according to the acquired cruise angle of attack.

The cruise angle of attack obtained from aerodynamic evaluation is 4.1 degrees. Although the total weight set in this paper is larger than that of most existing supersonic transport concepts, the cruise angle of attack is only 4.1 degrees, which improves passenger comfort to a certain extent. The cruise lift-to-drag ratio is 11.2. The lift coefficient is 0.107. The pressure drags and viscous drag account for 62.2% and 37.8% respectively, indicating that most of the drag is generated by volume, which is consistent with the basic assumption of large-sized SBWB. It can be seen from the pressure distribution (Figure 9) that the low-pressure region of SBWB appears earlier than that of the TAW configuration. The TAW configuration is usually designed with a very slender nose, which generates lift almost from the position close to half of the fuselage length. However, the non-lifting region of SBWB is only a part of the nose. The early generation of lift may provide a new direction for the forebody waveform design. In JSGD inverse design for TAW configurations, nose shock intensity generally needs to be enhanced. Stronger bow shock helps avoid shock coalescence. Earlier lift generation of SBWB enables part of the equivalent area at the nose to be contributed by aerodynamic lift, thereby reducing drag produced at the nose.

Figure 10 illustrates the near-field symmetry-plane overpressure distribution derived from sonic boom numerical analysis. The forebody pressure waveform of the proposed SBWB configuration exhibits a generally similar variation trend to that of the conventional TAW configuration. Nevertheless, constrained by the substantially extended cabin length of the large-capacity SBWB layout, the tapered transitional section of the rear fuselage is severely compressed on a longitudinal scale. The abrupt volumetric contraction of the aft fuselage induces intense local over-expansion flow, which constitutes a prominent unfavorable factor for low-boom design. In this regard, compared with conventional TAW supersonic schemes, the SBWB configuration faces more prominent afterbody waveform defects, indicating that subsequent low-boom optimization for the SBWB should focus primarily on the suppression and regulation of adverse afterbody wave characteristics.

Furthermore, the sonic boom signal extracted at a downstream distance of three times the fuselage length (R/L=3) beneath the aircraft is analyzed in detail. For comparative reference, the near-field waveform of conventionally designed configurations without low-boom optimization typically contains two intense shock waves, which are separately induced by the volumetric discontinuity at the nose and the lift discontinuity at wing leading edge. In contrast, the fore-body waveform of the baseline SBWB in this study exhibits a flat-top waveform. This waveform characteristic is presumably attributed to its extended lift distribution range and the axial coupling effect between volumetric variation and lift variation along the fuselage. In the traditional low-boom design of TAW configurations, the classic split design concept assumes that the modification of fuselage volume distribution exerts negligible influence on aircraft lift performance. However, the analytical results indicate that this conventional assumption is no longer applicable to the SBWB configuration, which further demonstrates the essential necessity of implementing integrated aerodynamic and low-boom design for SBWB.

Far-field propagation calculation based on near-field sonic boom waveforms reveals that the ground sonic boom intensity directly beneath the aircraft reaches 105.67 PLdB, a magnitude comparable to that of the Concorde airliner. Observations on the far-field waveform show that although its overall trend is close to a typical N-wave, the leading shock waves are not fully merged and present indistinct multiple shock wave characteristics. This phenomenon demonstrates that the overall sonic boom intensity of the SBWB configuration is mainly determined by shock wave merging and the large amplitude induced by high lift. Accordingly, the classic JSGD theory will be separately applied to conduct low-boom design on the SBWB model, to explore the effective potential of sonic boom suppression for this configuration.

# 4.    Result and Analysis

## 4.1    Fore Body Waveform Analysis and Inverse Design

Sonic-boom minimization theory, also referred to as shaped sonic-boom theory, was established by Jones[9], George[10] and Seebass[11], and Darden[12]. The core principle of the JSGD inverse design method is summarized as follows. First, based on the sonic-boom minimization theory and key flight parameters including the aircraft’s cruise altitude, fuselage length, and cruise weight, the low-boom optimized F-function with excellent sonic boom suppression performance is derived. Second, the target equivalent area distribution is calculated from the obtained F-function. Finally, aerodynamic configuration optimization is implemented to match the practical equivalent area distribution with the target distribution, thereby realizing effective sonic boom attenuation.

Although the fuselage length set in the baseline configuration generation stage is 72 meters, its effective length cut by the Mach surface should be used when generating the JSGD target due to the cruise angle of attack. This paper uses 68 meters as the effective length to generate the JSGD equivalent-area distribution target. Figure 12 shows the target F-function and comparison between the equivalent-area of baseline configuration and the target equivalent-area distribution adopted in this paper.

It can be observed from the equivalent-area distribution that the large-volume cabin dominates the overall equivalent-area variation. The 160-seat cabin structure is analyzed and modeled independently, with conical profiles adopted to substitute the nose and tail sections to keep the start and end positions of equivalent-area distribution consistent with those of the full aircraft configuration. Considering practical shapes must maintain convex characteristics, this profile serves as the lower limit of equivalent area under the designated cabin volume. The geometric boundary of minimum equivalent area is then enclosed by circular fuselage sections, and the relevant sectional area distribution is compared with that of the baseline SBWB configuration as illustrated in Figure 13. The comparison shows that the equivalent area of circular cabin sections is obviously larger than that of the SBWB structure. It demonstrates that circular fuselage forms impose tighter constraints on equivalent-area adjustment in large-sized SST design, which increases design difficulty and may even prevent the target distribution from being achieved.

This study conducts JSGD inverse design using the full-aircraft parameterization method presented in Section 3.1. Ten sections are defined with fifth-order CST parameters. The spanwise stations of all sections are kept fixed and excluded from design variables, yielding a total of 160 design variables in total.

To address slow convergence in high-dimensional optimization, the design is divided into two stages.

The first stage performs planform optimization with CST parameters of each section fixed. It optimizes leading/trailing edge positions, z-direction offsets and relative trailing edge offsets of individual sections, while keeping parameters at the symmetry plane unchanged. With 37 design variables, this stage minimizes the integral of deviations between actual and target equivalent-area distributions. It barely changes the aircraft volume and mainly aims to obtain an optimal lift distribution.

The second stage refines section shapes with the planform fixed, involving 120 design variables. A block-based strategy is adopted for this high-dimensional problem. A random subset of variables is selected to update the target volume equivalent-area, which requires no flow computation and incurs little cost. The differential evolution algorithm is used for sub-optimization. Results are accepted if the equivalent-area is improved; otherwise, they are discarded and new variables are sampled. In this work, the 120 variables are randomly split into 10 groups for sub-optimization. After regrouping and repeating the process three times, 30 iterations and lift evaluations are completed, with 20 accepted and 10 rejected.

The optimized configuration is evaluated for aerodynamic and sonic boom characteristics with high fidelity CFD method. Due to certain errors in the lift analysis using the panel method, the cruise angle of attack of the inverse‑designed configuration under high‑fidelity analysis is somewhat different from the panel method result, so the calculated sonic boom signature does not fully match the JSGD target waveform. We observe that the optimized rear-body waveform generates a relatively strong shock wave. This is mainly attributed to poor equivalent-area matching at the rear-body section. The rapid contraction of the empennage intensifies expansion waves, which to some extent inhibits the forward merging of shock waves formed at the fuselage trailing edge. It is worth noting that due to the use of relatively low‑order CST parameters for parameterization, the shape smoothness is high, so the waveform also shows high smoothness without small discontinuities commonly seen in the design results of conventional configurations. However, in classical sonic boom theory, these small discontinuities may help prevent compression wave merging and thus reduce sonic boom. To generate such features in the waveform, it may be necessary to further increase the order of CST and adopt effective methods to filter the strange shapes caused by the increase of CST order.

The far-field propagation calculation is performed on the sonic boom signal at three times the fuselage length to obtain the ground waveform and the PLdB distribution at circumferential angles. The sonic boom intensity directly below is reduced to 99.21 PLdB (Figure 16), which is 6.46 PLdB lower than that of the baseline configuration. It is worth noting that the sonic boom reduction effect is even better at positions with small azimuth angles than directly beneath the aircraft. This is because the overall volume is reduced to a certain extent during optimization, and the volume contribution from lateral positions gradually becomes dominant. Consequently, the boom mitigation performance is less constrained by lift requirements.

In this section, the JSGD inverse design method is adopted to carry out low-boom design for the SBWB configuration, and waveforms like theoretical waveform of minimum sonic boom. The results prove that classic low-boom design methods are still well applicable to the SBWB configuration.

## 4.2    Rear Body Waveform Analysis

The above low-boom design is fully implemented based on a tailless clean configuration. Although such tailless configuration have become a major research direction, especially in the design of hypersonic aircraft and supersonic fighters, they are generally regarded as unsuitable for civil airliners due to difficult control characteristics. Hence, it is necessary to add additional components to the basic configuration to take control performance into account. In addition, the analysis on sonic boom characteristics of the clean configuration reveals extremely strong expansion waves at the rear body, which cannot be effectively improved in subsequent low-boom design processes. In the low-boom design of TAW configurations, control surfaces are widely adopted as an effective way to adjust rear-body waveform. It is worth exploring whether this method can also be applied to the low-boom design of SBWB. Therefore, this paper selects winglets and V-tails, which are commonly used in BWB design, to study their influences on sonic boom signatures.

The dimensions of winglets and V-tails are calculated by empirical formulas. The planform sizes and installation positions of the two components are shown in Figure 17. Symmetric thin airfoils are adopted for V-tails to balance control efficiency and aerodynamic drag reduction, while winglets directly use the same airfoil as the wing tip.

Winglets and V-tails are separately installed on the optimized configuration for high-fidelity analysis. To ensure the influences of additional components act on the identical determined waveform obtained after JSGD inverse design, all sonic boom analyses are conducted at a fixed angle of attack.

It is observed that installing winglets alone slightly intensifies the shock wave formed at the terminal section of the volumetric distribution and alleviates the over-expansion phenomenon of the rear-body to a certain extent. Previous research has confirmed that wingtip vortices propagate downstream along the Mach cone and interact with the over-expansion flow field behind the rear-body shock. Such mutual interaction further amplifies the magnitude of over-expansion through wave superposition. On the one hand, additional flow disturbances induced by winglets superimpose onto the rear-body waveform, leading to a moderate rise in shock wave amplitude. On the other hand, winglets perform well in restraining intense wingtip flow disturbances and hinder the backward propagation of low-pressure flow over the wing upper surface, which prevents these flow features from interacting with the rear-body over-expansion region. Despite the above improvements, the overall suppression effect on over-expansion still fails to meet the anticipated design targets.

Further comparative analysis on the rear-body waveform of the clean configuration and the winglet-fitted model indicates that the shock wave generated at the fuselage tail is extremely strong. During downstream propagation, the majority of low-pressure disturbances originating from wingtip flow are intercepted by this robust rear-body shock and therefore cannot travel to the symmetry plane to form superimposed flow structures. This result demonstrates that wingtip vortex disturbances only take a small proportion of the factors triggering rear-body over-expansion, even for the original clean configuration. Accordingly, merely suppressing wingtip flow disturbances is insufficient to achieve desirable performance in sonic boom mitigation.

In contrast, simply equipping the aircraft with V-tails exerts a negligible influence on the waveform. Flow field analysis indicates that the symmetric V-tails with zero incidence angle generate minor flow disturbances, most of which are shielded by the fuselage and fail to affect the sonic boom waveform below, resulting in limited capability for wave system regulation. It is worth noting that V-tails with preset incidence angles are adopted in some existing designs to compensate for lift. As expected, such configurations produce stronger flow disturbances and may impose a more pronounced impact on the waveform.

Subsequently, winglets and V-tails are both mounted on the JSGD designed configuration for sonic boom characteristic analysis. According to the near-field waveform results, although V-tails exert negligible influence on the wave system when applied alone, the combined effect of the two components differs from the simple superposition of their individual performances after integrated installation. The rear-body over-expansion intensity generated by the coupling effect of the two components is further slightly weakened, and the shock wave enhancement induced by winglets is also suppressed to a certain extent. This indicates that a mutual interference effect exists between the flow disturbances produced by winglets and V-tails, which favorably improves the near-field wave system structure and achieves a partial weakening of the adverse aerodynamic wave characteristics. Near-field waveform signals at different azimuthal angles are propagated to the ground for far-field sonic boom evaluation. The calculation results demonstrate that the ground sonic boom intensity directly beneath the aircraft is reduced from 99.2 PLdB to 98.7 PLdB after the combined installation of winglets and V-tails, verifying the effective sonic boom suppression capability of the compound aerodynamic configuration.

This section investigates the effects of winglets and V-tails on the sonic boom characteristics of the SBWB configuration. The results show that winglets can effectively mitigate the over-expansion of the rear-body. V-tails deliver limited improvement when used alone, yet they can further enhance regulation performance when combined with winglets. It should be noted that the severe rear-body over-expansion of the present configuration may mask the effects of these components to some extent. Further research is still required to reveal the underlying mechanisms of how aerodynamic components affect the sonic boom characteristics of SBWB configuration.

# 5.    Conclusion

This paper discusses the aerodynamic feasibility and sonic boom feasibility of a 160-seat large-sized SBWB. It is found that the SBWB configuration features a smaller cruise angle of attack and a wider lift distribution range. And its fuselage fits the cabin structure better, which is conducive to equivalent area distribution matching in low-boom design. Based on the above research results, the classic JSGD inverse design method is adopted in sequence to conduct low-boom design for the SBWB, and the ground sonic boom intensity is reduced from 105.67PLdB to 98.7PLdB. Afterwards, the rear-body waveform of SBWB is analyzed, result shows that winglets can shield the expansion on the upper wing surface, which helps suppress sonic boom levels, while most of the impact of V-tails on the sonic boom waveform is blocked by the rear fuselage. It is undeniable that V-tails exert a notable influence on pressure waveforms. Rational design of V-tails can effectively achieve the purpose of wave system regulation. The research results show that large-sized SST has economic advantages and can achieve sonic boom mitigation through low-boom design.

Follow-up research can further adopt methods such as PNFO to design the fore-body waveform, and canards can be introduced for waveform control if necessary. For the rear-body, the over-expansion problem needs to be addressed first, and the research focus should remain on weakening the shock waves induced by volume termination, thereby reducing the intensity of over-expansion. This paper finds that the sonic boom characteristics of the SBWB configuration differ greatly from that of TAW configuration. In addition to further research on large-sized SBWB, attempts can also be made to apply the SBWB configuration to the design and research of supersonic business jets.
