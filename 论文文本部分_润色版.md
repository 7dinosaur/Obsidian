# Abstract
The development of next-generation supersonic transport (SST) represents a critical direction for future civil aviation. However, most existing SST configurations are characterized by small passenger capacity, which significantly limits their commercial application potential. The fuselage volume of the conventional tube-and-wing (TAW) configuration increases quadratically with the number of seats. To address this issue, a 160-seat large-sized supersonic blended-wing-body (SBWB) configuration is proposed in this paper, and a dedicated parametric method is established to generate the baseline configuration under cabin volume constraints. To characterize the sonic boom performance of the SBWB, systematic analyses are conducted on both fore-body and rear-body waveforms. The Jones–Seebass–George–Darden (JSGD) inverse design method is adopted for fore-body waveform optimization, reducing the ground perceived loudness level (PLdB) of the SBWB by 6.46. Rear-body waveform analysis reveals prominent over-expansion phenomena for large-sized SBWB, which restricts further sonic boom reduction. Therefore, the regulation effect of aerodynamic auxiliary components on the rear-body waveform is investigated. The results indicate that winglets can weaken the intensity of rear-body over-expansion and deliver waveform regulation benefits to a certain degree, while the control effect of V-tails is rather limited; the sonic boom intensity is further reduced to 98.7 PLdB. Overall, the 160-seat SBWB exhibits aerodynamic advantages including a low cruise angle of attack and a broad lift distribution, and demonstrates promising low-boom potential at large passenger capacity, providing a reference for the low-boom design of future large-sized SST configurations.

# 1.    Introduction
Higher travel speed has long been a pursuit of humanity. The flight speed of supersonic transport is twice that of traditional subsonic airliners or even higher, which significantly improves travel efficiency and facilitates global economic and cultural exchanges. Therefore, the new-generation SST represents a core technological frontier in the aviation industry of the 21st century[1-2]. In its aviation strategic plan released in 2023, NASA stated that "the development of efficient, economical and eco-friendly supersonic commercial transportation will become a gamechanger for intercontinental travel in the future"[3]. In 2025, the White House signed a presidential executive order titled "Leading the World in Supersonic Flight" to boost the development of domestic supersonic civil air transportation.

After decades of research, various advanced low-boom SST configurations have been developed across different countries, such as NASA X-59[4], Lockheed Martin QSTA[5], TsAGI Strizh of Russia[6] and JAXA S4 of Japan[7]. Nevertheless, most of these designs are intended for small-capacity supersonic business jets. The X-59, which delivers the optimal sonic boom suppression, carries merely a single passenger and serves solely as a demonstrator for sonic boom research. While small supersonic business jet concepts benefit from certain market demand and can more readily satisfy low sonic boom limits, supersonic transport imposes stringent airport requirements and consumes far more fuel than subsonic airliners, leading to exorbitant operating costs. To offset such heavy costs, airfares would surge considerably, making these aircraft inaccessible to most travelers. Expanding seating capacity represents one of the most direct approaches to improving economic efficiency. Distributing fixed operational expenditures including airport fees and fuel costs across a larger passenger pool can reduce ticket prices while increasing overall economic returns. Over the past few decades, research into low-boom design has matured steadily within academic circles. Geometry shaping is the most widely adopted and effective design technique[2]. Driven by the demands of aerodynamic configuration design, a set of powerful inverse design algorithms including JSGD[8-11], LSG[12-14] and PNFO[15] have been developed. These advances render the low-boom design of large-sized SST a viable prospect.

The fuselage of TAW configurations is characterized by low volumetric efficiency. When such configurations are adopted for large-sized supersonic airliners, stringent volumetric constraints considerably increase the complexity of low-boom design. Innovative configurations for supersonic civil aircraft have also been investigated in recent years, among which the bidirectional flying wing configuration[16-17] is a representative example. However, its rotating mechanism introduces unprecedented structural challenges for the passenger cabin and does not deliver tangible improvements in commercial profitability in practice. By contrast, the blended-wing-body (BWB) configuration largely utilizes the proven component architecture of conventional aircraft[18-19]. Beyond its superior aerodynamic performance, the non-circular central fuselage of the BWB conforms closely to cabin contours, which partially mitigates the volumetric penalties incurred by increased seating capacity and facilitates low-boom optimization.

Accordingly, a 160-seat large SBWB configuration is proposed in this study. Its aerodynamic and sonic boom characteristics are analyzed, and the JSGD inverse design method is applied to fore-body waveform design. After subsequent rear-body waveform assessment, the effects of winglets and V-tails on wave profiles are investigated.

# 2.    Methodology

## 2.1    Aerodynamic analysis

Aerodynamic and high-fidelity sonic boom analyses in this study are performed using high-precision CFD numerical approaches. The Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations combined with the turbulence model constitute the governing equations. Zonal meshes composed of cylindrical near-field and conical far-field domains are adopted for high-fidelity sonic boom calculation. Grid lines of the conical mesh are arranged along the Mach angle to suppress numerical dissipation in sonic boom propagation.

The JAXA-Wing-Body (JWB) standard model from the 2nd AIAA Sonic Boom Prediction Workshop (SBPW) is employed for numerical method validation. Figure 1 presents the geometric configuration and sonic boom mesh topology of the JWB model, with a total mesh cell count of 17 million.

The calculation results are compared with data contributed by workshop participants (Figure 2). The near-field pressure waveform exhibits favorable consistency with reference results. Discrepancies observed in the rear-body waveform primarily originate from intricate local flow features, which induce noticeable deviations among different research outputs. Even so, the overall variation trend of the obtained results aligns well with published datasets. Aerodynamic force predictions also demonstrate satisfactory agreement with official benchmark values. The validation confirms the reliability of the adopted CFD method, which can be applied to further detailed flow and wave characteristic analysis.

## 2.2    Sonic Boom Prediction

The sonic boom calculation methods applied in this study comprise two categories. The low-fidelity modified Whitham linear theory is utilized for rapid low-boom design. The far-field propagation method based on the Augmented Burgers equation is integrated with the near-field overpressure waveform calculated by CFD, allowing high-fidelity sonic boom analysis covering both near and far fields.

*2.2.1*    *Rapid Sonic Boom Prediction*

The JWB model is adopted to validate the rapid sonic boom prediction method derived from modified Whitham linear theory. The corresponding F-function is calculated from the equivalent-area distribution to acquire near-field sonic boom characteristics, while the lift distribution is solved using the high-order panel method. Comparisons between predicted signatures and CFD outcomes are presented in Figure 3. Results from the rapid method follow essentially identical variation trends to those of high-fidelity calculations, and the attained accuracy satisfies design demands focused on forebody low-boom optimization.

# 3.    Baseline SBWB Configuration

## 3.1    Parametric Method and Baseline Configuration Generation

The TAW fuselage diameter is constrained by cabin size, and its sectional area increases quadratically with the number of seats per row. For large SSTs, this leads to a substantial increase in fuselage volume and raises the difficulty of sonic boom design. In comparison, the BWB adopts a non-circular fuselage that conforms more closely to the cabin, providing a more compact volume distribution and lower volume penalty at the same seating capacity (Figure 5). However, the high integration of the cabin and wing renders the traditional split design framework inapplicable, thereby requiring a full-aircraft parametric method dedicated to the SBWB configuration.

To address this issue, a full-aircraft CST parametric method (Figure 6) is adopted in this study to ensure the global geometric continuity and smoothness of the configuration during optimization. In this approach, the SBWB geometry is defined by a set of closed sectional curves, where all sections are parameterized using CST functions of identical order. Third-order NURBS interpolation is employed between adjacent sections to construct aerodynamic surfaces and maintain second-order geometric continuity. Additional design parameters include the streamwise coordinates of leading/trailing edges as well as the vertical offset of each section. Low-boom design implemented within this unified parametric framework eliminates the need for repeated FFD box reconstruction and re-parameterization induced by geometric deformation.

During surface generation, CST parameters are constructed based on Bernstein polynomials with infinite-order differentiability everywhere, which preserves the G2 continuity of streamwise grids for all sections. Similarly, cubic NURBS surfaces can theoretically achieve inherent G2 continuity without extra modification. Accordingly, the present parametric method enables full-aircraft surfaces to maintain continuous G2 characteristics throughout the configuration. On this basis, additional constraints are imposed on the spanwise surface geometry to avoid structurally infeasible shapes in engineering practice. Total curvature variation and second-derivative inflection criteria are adopted in this work as practical engineering constraints.

The cabin envelope is established as the primary geometric constraint for SBWB parametric modeling. A 160-seat layout with 40 rows and four seats per row is configured, and major cabin dimensions are specified to meet passenger comfort requirements. Due to the limited internal space of supersonic transport, overhead luggage racks are eliminated to prevent spatial crowding. Baggage storage is instead arranged in the tapered section of the rear fuselage.

The longitudinal symmetry-plane contour is generated according to the cabin boundary dimensions. The overall aircraft length is configured with a reserved height margin to compensate for geometric shrinkage in the spanwise direction. Key control points at the front, rear, upper and lower boundaries of the cabin symmetry plane are selected, and a CST curve is fitted to construct the baseline profile. During the baseline configuration design, offsets are applied to the nose and tail to increase the effective aircraft length, providing a favorable geometric foundation for subsequent low-boom optimization.

A 3%-thickness thin airfoil is applied uniformly to the outboard wing sections away from the cabin. The sectional profiles of the middle fuselage are acquired by interpolating the airfoil parameters between the outboard wing and the symmetry plane, and the initial aerodynamic configuration is thus constructed.

With flight efficiency and sonic boom characteristics taken into account, the cruise condition adopted in this study is set as a cruise altitude of 18 km and a cruise Mach number of 1.8, with a total SBWB weight of 150 t. To match the lift of the shape with the design weight, the leading-edge curve is further parameterized by a cubic polynomial, and a rapid lift-increasing optimization is carried out on the initial shape, thereby yielding the baseline configuration of this paper (Figure 8).

## 3.2    Aerodynamic Analysis of the Baseline Configuration

The aerodynamic and sonic boom characteristics of the baseline configuration are analyzed in this paper. As the panel method constitutes the lift assessment tool in primary rapid optimization, the cruise angle of attack is first evaluated via high-fidelity calculation with a relatively coarse aerodynamic mesh. The sonic boom mesh is then generated according to the acquired cruise angle of attack.

The cruise angle of attack obtained from aerodynamic evaluation is 4.1 degrees. Although the total weight set in this paper is larger than that of most existing supersonic transport concepts, the cruise angle of attack is merely 4.1 degrees, which improves passenger comfort to a certain extent. The cruise lift-to-drag ratio is 11.2. The lift coefficient is 0.107. Pressure drag and viscous drag account for 62.2% and 37.8% respectively, indicating that the majority of the drag is generated by volume, which is consistent with the basic assumption of large-sized SBWB. As shown in the pressure distribution (Figure 9), the low-pressure region of the SBWB appears earlier than that of the TAW configuration. The TAW configuration is typically designed with a very slender nose, which generates lift approximately from a position near the half-length of the fuselage. However, the non-lifting region of the SBWB is limited to only a portion of the nose. The early generation of lift may provide a new direction for forebody waveform design. In JSGD inverse design for TAW configurations, the nose shock intensity generally must be enhanced, as a stronger bow shock serves to prevent shock coalescence. The earlier lift generation of the SBWB allows part of the equivalent area at the nose to be contributed by aerodynamic lift, thereby reducing drag produced at the nose.

Figure 10 illustrates the near-field symmetry-plane overpressure distribution derived from sonic boom numerical analysis. The forebody pressure waveform of the proposed SBWB configuration exhibits a generally similar variation trend to that of the conventional TAW configuration. Nevertheless, constrained by the substantially extended cabin length of the large-capacity SBWB layout, the tapered transitional section of the rear fuselage is severely compressed in the longitudinal direction. The abrupt volumetric contraction of the aft fuselage induces intense local over-expansion flow, which constitutes a prominent unfavorable factor for low-boom design. In this regard, compared with conventional TAW supersonic schemes, the SBWB configuration exhibits more pronounced afterbody waveform defects, indicating that subsequent low-boom optimization for the SBWB should focus primarily on the suppression and regulation of adverse afterbody wave characteristics.

Furthermore, the sonic boom signal extracted at a downstream distance of three times the fuselage length (R/L = 3) beneath the aircraft is analyzed in detail. For comparative reference, the near-field waveform of conventionally designed configurations without low-boom optimization typically contains two intense shock waves, which are separately induced by the volumetric discontinuity at the nose and the lift discontinuity at the wing leading edge. In contrast, the fore-body waveform of the baseline SBWB in this study exhibits a flat-top profile. This waveform characteristic is attributed to its extended lift distribution range and the axial coupling effect between volumetric variation and lift variation along the fuselage. In the traditional low-boom design of TAW configurations, the classic split design concept assumes that the modification of fuselage volume distribution exerts negligible influence on aircraft lift performance. However, the analytical results indicate that this conventional assumption is no longer applicable to the SBWB configuration, which further demonstrates the necessity of implementing integrated aerodynamic and low-boom design for the SBWB.

Far-field propagation calculation based on near-field sonic boom waveforms reveals that the ground sonic boom intensity directly beneath the aircraft reaches 105.67 PLdB, a magnitude comparable to that of the Concorde airliner. Observations of the far-field waveform indicate that although its overall trend is close to a typical N-wave, the leading shock waves are not fully merged and display indistinct multiple shock wave characteristics. This phenomenon demonstrates that the overall sonic boom intensity of the SBWB configuration is primarily determined by shock wave merging and the large amplitude induced by high lift. Accordingly, the classic JSGD theory is separately applied to conduct low-boom design on the SBWB model, to explore the effective potential of sonic boom suppression for this configuration.

# 4.    Result and Analysis

## 4.1    Fore-Body Waveform Analysis and Inverse Design

Sonic-boom minimization theory, also referred to as shaped sonic-boom theory, was established by Jones[9], George[10], Seebass[11], and Darden[12]. The core principle of the JSGD inverse design method is summarized as follows. First, based on the sonic-boom minimization theory and key flight parameters including the aircraft's cruise altitude, fuselage length, and cruise weight, the low-boom optimized F-function with excellent sonic boom suppression performance is derived. Second, the target equivalent-area distribution is calculated from the obtained F-function. Finally, aerodynamic configuration optimization is implemented to match the practical equivalent-area distribution with the target distribution, thereby realizing effective sonic boom attenuation.

Although the fuselage length set in the baseline configuration generation stage is 72 meters, its effective length cut by the Mach surface must be used when generating the JSGD target due to the cruise angle of attack. An effective length of 68 meters is adopted in this paper to generate the JSGD equivalent-area distribution target. Figure 12 shows the target F-function and a comparison between the equivalent-area distribution of the baseline configuration and the target equivalent-area distribution adopted in this paper.

It is evident from the equivalent-area distribution that the large-volume cabin governs the overall equivalent-area variation. The 160-seat cabin structure is analyzed and modeled independently, with conical profiles adopted to approximate the nose and tail sections so as to keep the start and end positions of the equivalent-area distribution consistent with those of the full aircraft configuration. Since practical shapes must maintain convex characteristics, this profile serves as the lower limit of equivalent area under the designated cabin volume. The geometric boundary of minimum equivalent area is then enclosed by circular fuselage sections, and the relevant sectional area distribution is compared with that of the baseline SBWB configuration as illustrated in Figure 13. The comparison indicates that the equivalent area of circular cabin sections is substantially larger than that of the SBWB structure. This demonstrates that circular fuselage forms impose more stringent constraints on equivalent-area adjustment in large-sized SST design, which increases design difficulty and may even prevent the target distribution from being achieved.

JSGD inverse design is conducted in this study using the full-aircraft parameterization method presented in Section 3.1. Ten sections are defined with fifth-order CST parameters. The spanwise stations of all sections are kept fixed and excluded from design variables, yielding a total of 160 design variables.

To address slow convergence in high-dimensional optimization, the design is divided into two stages.

The first stage performs planform optimization with the CST parameters of each section fixed. The leading/trailing edge positions, z-direction offsets and relative trailing edge offsets of individual sections are optimized, while parameters at the symmetry plane remain unchanged. With 37 design variables, this stage minimizes the integral of deviations between actual and target equivalent-area distributions. It barely changes the aircraft volume and mainly aims to obtain an optimal lift distribution.

The second stage refines section shapes with the planform fixed, involving 120 design variables. A block-based strategy is adopted: random subsets are selected to update the target equivalent-area without flow computation, and the differential evolution algorithm is employed for sub-optimization. Only results that improve the equivalent-area are retained. The 120 variables are split into 10 groups; after three regrouping cycles, 20 of 30 iterations are accepted. Figure 14 presents the optimized equivalent-area distribution and the near-field waveform obtained from the rapid sonic boom prediction method.

The optimized configuration is evaluated for aerodynamic and sonic boom characteristics using the high-fidelity CFD method. Due to inherent discrepancies in the lift analysis performed by the panel method, the cruise angle of attack of the inverse-designed configuration under high-fidelity analysis deviates from the panel method result, and consequently the calculated sonic boom signature does not fully match the JSGD target waveform. It is observed that the optimized rear-body waveform generates a relatively strong shock wave. This is primarily attributed to insufficient equivalent-area matching at the rear-body section. The rapid contraction of the empennage intensifies expansion waves, which to a certain extent inhibits the forward merging of shock waves formed at the fuselage trailing edge. It is worth noting that due to the adoption of relatively low-order CST parameters for parameterization, the shape smoothness is high, and the waveform accordingly exhibits high smoothness without the small discontinuities commonly observed in the design results of conventional configurations. However, in classical sonic boom theory, these small discontinuities may serve to prevent compression wave merging and thus reduce sonic boom. To generate such features in the waveform, it may be necessary to further increase the order of CST and adopt effective methods to filter the irregular shapes caused by the increase of CST order.

The far-field propagation calculation is performed on the sonic boom signal at three times the fuselage length to obtain the ground waveform and the PLdB distribution at circumferential angles. The sonic boom intensity directly below is reduced to 99.21 PLdB (Figure 16), which is 6.46 PLdB lower than that of the baseline configuration. It is worth noting that the sonic boom reduction effect is more pronounced at positions with small azimuth angles than directly beneath the aircraft. This arises because the overall volume is reduced to a certain extent during optimization, and the volume contribution from lateral positions gradually becomes dominant. Consequently, the boom mitigation performance is less constrained by lift requirements.

In this section, the JSGD inverse design method is adopted to carry out low-boom design for the SBWB configuration, yielding waveforms that approach the theoretical waveform of minimum sonic boom. The results demonstrate that classic low-boom design methods remain well applicable to the SBWB configuration.

## 4.2    Rear-Body Waveform Analysis

The preceding low-boom design is implemented based on a tailless clean configuration. Although such tailless configurations have emerged as a major research direction, especially in the design of hypersonic aircraft and supersonic fighters, they are generally regarded as unsuitable for civil airliners due to challenging control characteristics. Therefore, it is necessary to incorporate additional components into the basic configuration to account for control performance. In addition, the analysis of sonic boom characteristics of the clean configuration reveals exceptionally strong expansion waves at the rear body, which cannot be effectively mitigated in subsequent low-boom design processes. In the low-boom design of TAW configurations, control surfaces are widely adopted as an effective means of adjusting the rear-body waveform. Whether this method can also be applied to the low-boom design of the SBWB merits further investigation. Therefore, winglets and V-tails, which are commonly used in BWB design, are selected in this paper to study their influences on sonic boom signatures.

The dimensions of winglets and V-tails are calculated using empirical formulas. The planform sizes and installation positions of the two components are shown in Figure 17. Symmetric thin airfoils are adopted for V-tails to balance control efficiency and aerodynamic drag reduction, while winglets employ the same airfoil as the wing tip.

Winglets and V-tails are separately installed on the optimized configuration for high-fidelity analysis. To ensure that the influences of additional components are evaluated on the identical prescribed waveform obtained after JSGD inverse design, all sonic boom analyses are conducted at a fixed angle of attack.

It is observed that installing winglets alone slightly intensifies the shock wave formed at the terminal section of the volumetric distribution and alleviates the over-expansion phenomenon of the rear-body to a certain degree. Previous research has confirmed that wingtip vortices propagate downstream along the Mach cone and interact with the over-expansion flow field behind the rear-body shock. Such mutual interaction further amplifies the magnitude of over-expansion through wave superposition. On the one hand, additional flow disturbances induced by winglets superimpose onto the rear-body waveform, leading to a moderate rise in shock wave amplitude. On the other hand, winglets are effective in restraining intense wingtip flow disturbances and hinder the backward propagation of low-pressure flow over the wing upper surface, which prevents these flow features from interacting with the rear-body over-expansion region. Despite the above improvements, the overall suppression effect on over-expansion still does not meet the anticipated design targets.

Further comparative analysis of the rear-body waveform of the clean configuration and the winglet-fitted model indicates that the shock wave generated at the fuselage tail is exceptionally strong. During downstream propagation, the majority of low-pressure disturbances originating from wingtip flow are intercepted by this robust rear-body shock and therefore cannot travel to the symmetry plane to form superimposed flow structures. This result demonstrates that wingtip vortex disturbances constitute only a small proportion of the factors triggering rear-body over-expansion, even for the original clean configuration. Accordingly, merely suppressing wingtip flow disturbances is insufficient to achieve desirable performance in sonic boom mitigation.

In contrast, equipping the aircraft with V-tails alone exerts a negligible influence on the waveform. Flow field analysis indicates that the symmetric V-tails with zero incidence angle generate minor flow disturbances, most of which are shielded by the fuselage and cannot affect the sonic boom waveform below, resulting in limited capability for wave system regulation. It is worth noting that V-tails with preset incidence angles are adopted in some existing designs to compensate for lift. As anticipated, such configurations produce stronger flow disturbances and may exert a more pronounced impact on the waveform.

Subsequently, winglets and V-tails are both mounted on the JSGD-designed configuration for sonic boom characteristic analysis. According to the near-field waveform results, although V-tails exert negligible influence on the wave system when applied alone, the combined effect of the two components differs from the simple superposition of their individual contributions after integrated installation. The rear-body over-expansion intensity generated by the coupling effect of the two components is further marginally reduced, and the shock wave enhancement induced by winglets is also suppressed to a certain extent. This indicates that a mutual interference effect exists between the flow disturbances produced by winglets and V-tails, which improves the near-field wave system structure and achieves a partial reduction of the adverse aerodynamic wave characteristics. Near-field waveform signals at different azimuthal angles are propagated to the ground for far-field sonic boom evaluation. The calculation results demonstrate that the ground sonic boom intensity directly beneath the aircraft is reduced from 99.2 PLdB to 98.7 PLdB after the combined installation of winglets and V-tails, confirming the effective sonic boom suppression capability of the compound aerodynamic configuration.

This section has investigated the effects of winglets and V-tails on the sonic boom characteristics of the SBWB configuration. The results indicate that winglets can effectively mitigate rear-body over-expansion. V-tails provide limited improvement when used alone, yet they further enhance regulation performance when combined with winglets. It should be noted that the severe rear-body over-expansion of the present configuration may mask the effects of these components to some extent. Further research is required to reveal the underlying mechanisms by which aerodynamic components affect the sonic boom characteristics of the SBWB configuration.

# 5.    Conclusion

The aerodynamic feasibility and low-boom feasibility of a 160-seat large-sized SBWB are examined in this paper. It is found that the SBWB configuration is characterized by a smaller cruise angle of attack and a wider lift distribution range. Moreover, its fuselage conforms more closely to the cabin structure, which facilitates equivalent-area distribution matching in low-boom design. Based on the preceding research results, the classic JSGD inverse design method is subsequently adopted to conduct low-boom design for the SBWB, and the ground sonic boom intensity is reduced from 105.67 PLdB to 98.7 PLdB. The rear-body waveform of the SBWB is then analyzed; the results indicate that winglets can shield the expansion on the upper wing surface, which contributes to suppressing sonic boom levels, while most of the impact of V-tails on the sonic boom waveform is blocked by the rear fuselage. It is acknowledged that V-tails exert a notable influence on pressure waveforms, and rational design of V-tails can effectively achieve the objective of wave system regulation. The research results demonstrate that large-sized SST possesses economic advantages and can achieve sonic boom mitigation through low-boom design.

Future research may further adopt methods such as PNFO to design the fore-body waveform, and canards may be introduced for waveform control if necessary. For the rear-body, the over-expansion problem must be addressed first, and the research focus should remain on weakening the shock waves induced by volume termination, thereby reducing the intensity of over-expansion. The findings of this paper indicate that the sonic boom characteristics of the SBWB configuration differ substantially from those of the TAW configuration. In addition to further research on large-sized SBWB, the application of the SBWB configuration to the design and research of supersonic business jets may also be explored.
